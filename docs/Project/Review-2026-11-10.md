## 1. Summary

1. **Five of the seven tasks can be signed off with follow-ups; two are not ready**, each by small fixes: MYLA-359 (lazy parsing can load a different program without an error) and MYLA-397 (works only when the file and the library root are spelled the same way).
2. **Lazy parsing is sound where it claims to be, and unsafe at its edge.** The generated code is byte-identical to the eager compiler's for the compiler itself and 105 of 117 programs, error positions inside late-read bodies are unchanged, and the numbers reproduce (hello world 100.7 M to 39.4 M instructions, 24.9 to 9.6 ms). But a method with a missing `}` silently swallows the methods after it when the file's braces balance overall, and an unread body is an empty block with no marker, so two different mistakes compiled to programs that do nothing or crash instead of failing.
3. **D177 (streams in bytes only) is complete and correct**: 320,000 differential stream commands on four runners, no mismatch, nothing left half-converted. It regressed two things it didn't measure: interpreted bulk I/O is 50 to 78 times slower, and a short read keeps a cell as large as the request, which is real memory on wasm32.
4. **The literal work (MYLA-401) is clean**: about 150 literal shapes, no hole reached a back end. Its reviewer found an older bug in the function it rewrote: `[1, 300, 3u8]` type-checks, and `a[1]` is 300 interpreted and 44 natively.
5. **Part 1's findings are all still open on this master.** I re-ran its sixteen main reproducers: none changed. The rewrite of `std.io` did not touch the quadratic `read_line` (16 MB line: 26 s).
6. **Baseline**: bootstrap fixed point; full suite 1,401 tests, 1 error, 334 skipped. The error is an aarch64 stress program (`t27_children_read_as_they_write`) that hit its 120 s timeout under the reviewers' load; alone the test passes in 40 s. One of my first 30 repeat runs also hung (60 s for a 0.13 s program), then 0 of 640 on aarch64 with the new and the old compiler and 0 of 600 on x86-64: a rare hang under qemu and load, older than round 4, worth a core dump on timeout in the test. `#test` coverage of `selfhost/` is 66 % (67 % before the round).

## 4. The five questions

### 1. D178: `c.keys` the field, `c.keys()` the method

**Take it as built, with one amendment, and write the row.**

- Keeping D45 forbids more than getters. On the pre-round-4 compiler, `#derive(Eq, Hash)` on `struct Commit { hash: str }` fails with "method 'hash' collides with a field", and so does `impl Format` on a struct with a field `format`. With no orphan rule, a module also couldn't implement a trait for another module's type whose field has the method's name.
- Rust's whole rule isn't available: the parser drops parentheses, so `(h.on)(x)` can't mean the field, and the silent retargeting of L1 would become the general case.
- The rule as built held on four runners over derives (fields named `eq`, `hash`, `to_json`, `cmp`, `format`, `len`, `keys`, `get`), actors, traits and bounds, generics, other modules' impls, pipes, partial application, `become` and compound assignment. Adding a method beside a data field changes no program; removing one turns its calls into an error with a help line.
- **The amendment** closes L1, the one silent case: where `x.f(…)` finds a method and the receiver's field `f` holds a function at that instantiation, make it an error at the call ("bind the field first: `let g = x.f`"). Today that field's function is reachable only through such a binding anyway.
- D178 has no row yet (the page still names D178 as the next number), and D45 needs its italic "refined by" note. Two sentences are stale: `Functions-and-closures.md` ("no method may then have its name") and `Architecture/Type-checker.md` line 42.
- What stays a trap: fields are public, so `c.len` and `c.len()` both check from anywhere and may differ (the task's own program prints 10 and 3). That is an argument for field privacy (part 1, decision 2), not against D178.

### 2. D170's row extended in place

**Keep it in the row; fix the page's own rule and the row's reason.**

- The page's rule is about changing a decision. MYLA-398 changes nothing D170 decided; it applies it to one more position, as an impl's trait already had it.
- There is precedent for extending in place: D89 (MYLA-351), D152, D124 ("refined 2026-11-04"), D107, D92. New numbers went to choices between alternatives.
- What is missing: the Rationale cell still gives the reason of 7 November, which wasn't about bounds, and the title says "in a type position". Add a dated clause to the reason and "or a bound" to the title.
- The introduction contradicts the practice. Add a sentence: a row may gain a dated clause when a later task applies the decision to a case it left out; a change of what was decided gets a new row and an italic note on the old one. D178 is of the second kind.
- Lazy parsing has no decision number at all, though the owner decided it twice (checking, then parsing). It deserves a row, with the answer to question 4.

### 3. `TcpStream`'s string methods

**Keep them removed. The real gap is that a socket has no text layer in async code, and it had none before either.**

- The removed `read_str_async(n)` gave a chunk, not a line. The reviewer wrote four socket programs before and after (a line echo server for 20 connections, a PING client, an HTTP/1.0 GET, a chat relay): each needs the same hand-written line buffer both times, and the difference is 1 to 6 lines and three or four conversions.
- `io.buffered(s).read_line_async()` doesn't type-check: `'net.TcpStream' does not satisfy the bound 'R: Pollable'`. And the blocking `buffered(s).read_line()`, which Net.md's table suggests, hangs in tasks unless there are more workers than blocked connections (it times out interpreted and with one worker).
- A generic `io.write_str(w, s)` can't be written today: the function gets a copy of the stream, so a caller's `MemoryStream` stays empty. Bringing the two methods back on `TcpStream` would invite the same on `File`, `FdStream` and `ChildStdin`.
- What closes the gap, in two small steps:
  1. **An async line reader for sockets.** `impl Pollable for TcpStream` is five lines and worked in the reviewer's trial (1,000 lines over 20 connections, three runners; the echo server shrinks from 123 to 82 lines). It calls a socket handle a descriptor, against std.net's rule for a Windows port; a reader of std.net's own, about 40 lines, avoids that.
  2. **`s.bytes()` on `str`** in the implicit std.str, so text to any stream is `w.write(line.bytes())` without an import.
- Net.md needs a line-protocol example and a warning that `buffered(s)` blocks its worker.
- Underneath is a language gap worth its own decision: a trait method can't be async, so there is no async `Read` or `Write`, and every async stream API is written per type.
- I would bring back `write_str_async` and add `read_line_async` on `TcpStream` only if both steps above are ruled out.

### 4. Lazy parsing for any library, or only the built-in one

**Keep it for any library, once Z1 and Z2 are fixed.**

- The burn suite and every contributor run with `--stdlib lib`. Limiting laziness to the built-in library would leave the path users run to two special tests, and cost every tool run in the repository 61 M instructions again.
- A reached body gives the eager message, kind and position byte for byte (eleven kinds of error compared). Of 32 kinds of broken body, 11 fall back to the eager error at load and 20 defer the identical error; the exceptions are Z1 and Z4.
- What still catches a broken body nobody calls: `myla check` or `myla test` of the module itself, any std module that imports it as the entry, the language server with the file open, and `test_std_modules` in CI. What doesn't: a program run with `--stdlib`, `scripts/bootstrap.sh` (compiling the compiler against a broken library exits 0), and the embed tool.
- The fixes that make "any library" safe:
  - Z1: fall back to eager lexing when a skipped body holds a declaration keyword at the start of a line (Myla has no nested `fun`), so the skip can't run past its method.
  - Z2: pack the place the limit tests.
  - An unread body carries a marker instead of being an empty block, and using one is an internal error (this also fixes Z3 and guards question 5).
  - `scripts/update-seed.sh` checks the seven implicit modules whole; the parity test fails on a `lib/std` file that reports `same 0`; one line in [[Development/Conventions]] tells library editors to run `myla test` on the module they changed.
- I would revisit only if contributors are actually bitten, and then with an environment variable for an eager run, not a different default.

### 5. MYLA-413: an imported std module's bodies read once called

**Yes for the standard library, never for a program's own modules, and not yet: three things come first.**

- The cost is real. `from std.io import println` adds 61.8 M instructions (lexing and parsing `io`, `fs` and `bytes` whole 29.9 M, checking their bodies 22.7 M, renaming 4.5 M). My own measurement of what one import adds to a hello world's check: std.io 16 ms, std.net 35 ms, std.http 95 ms.
- The reviewer prototyped it with two loader edits (`proto_413_loader.diff`): hello with std.io 101 M to 64 M (24 to 15 ms), with std.http 394 M to 180 M (105 to 45 ms).
- **The same prototype miscompiles one of 37 examples**: `slices_and_formats.my` builds and then segfaults. The lowering asks for `std$fmt$…`, `std$slice$…` and `std$task$cancel` by name, the checker never "called" them, and an unread body compiles as an empty function. The seven implicit modules have no such functions, which is why round 4 is safe.
- So, in this order:
  1. The marker for unread bodies (question 4), so that a missing body fails the build instead of running.
  2. One list of the std functions the back ends name, marked as called.
  3. The cheap structural fix: `std.io` imports `std.fs` only for `IoError` and `from_errno`, and the two import each other. Moving those into a small module of their own reads 36 KB instead of 81 KB for the commonest import of all, an estimated 101 M to about 60 M with no change of meaning.
  Then MYLA-413 for the standard library, with MYLA-412's signature cache beside it.
- Not for a program's own modules: `myla check` would come to mean "the reachable part type-checks", nobody runs `myla test` on each of their modules, and Z1's shape would have no CI behind it. Per-module caching, where each module is still checked whole, is the way to make those fast.
